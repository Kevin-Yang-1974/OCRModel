ABSTRACT Digitized methodologies in the recent era contribute to various fields of automation that used
to hold different interests and meanings of human life. Buildings with historical significance, cultural values,
and beliefs are becoming an interdisciplinary field of interest, engaging more computer scientists nowadays.
Such structures need more attention towards reconstructing their values using a flavor of computerized tools
instead of brickwork directly. Due to the wear of time, the tiles and engravings of most of the historical
monuments are on the verge of ruin, endangering significant historical values. In this survey, we rebuild the
values by delving deep into the device and methodologies by providing a comprehensive understanding of
emerging fields and some experimental decisions. We discuss heritage restoration from some essential papers
on 3D reconstruction, image inpainting, IoT-based methods, genetic algorithms, and image processing.
The survey explains Machine Learning, Deep Learning, and Computer Vision-based methods for various
restoration tasks in the related field. We divide this into certain parts contributing to different fields that
restore cultural heritage. Moreover, we infer that the techniques will be faster, cheaper, and more beneficial
to the context of image reconstruction in the near future.

II. DATA ACQUISITION FROM HERITAGE BUILDINGS
A. PHOTOGRAMMETRY AND 3D SCANNING
Photogrammetry (PH), ‘‘Die Photometrographie’’, is a tech-
nique to collect reliable information about an object by mea-
suring distinct features using recording, photo capturing, and
electromagnetic radiant imagery [6], [7]. It is the science
of making measurements from photographs. This technique
involves unique measurements of real-life 2D images for
digitized 3D paradigms. The following are the steps involved
in photogrammetry-based data collection:
• Image acquisition: Photogrammetry relies on pho-
tographs to generate 3D models. The photographs
should be taken using a calibrated camera and should
overlap sufficiently to allow for reconstruction. Accord-
ing to [79], planning is critical to ensure that the survey
area is divided into sections to ensure that all areas are
captured by the camera.
• Image preprocessing: The photographs should be pre-
processed to remove any distortions that may affect the
accuracy of the final 3D model.
• Image matching: The photographs should be matched
to identify common features and to generate correspon-
dences between them. This step is crucial in generating
accurate 3D models.
• Point cloud generation: Once the correspondences are
identified, a point cloud [80], [81] is generated using
triangulation. This point cloud represents the 3D coordi-
nates of the corresponding features in the photographs.
• Surface generation: A surface is generated from the
point cloud using various techniques, such as interpo-
lation, meshing, or contouring.
B. LASER SCANNING
Laser scanning [82], [83] is a non-contact method of collect-
ing 3D data using laser light. The technology known as laser
scanning, commonly referred to as LiDAR (Light Detection
and Ranging), employs lasers to collect information about the
size and shape of objects and settings. Map-making, survey-
ing, and the creation of 3D models of things and locations are
all common uses for this technology. The inspected region
can subsequently be represented in 3D or on a map using
these point clouds. The following are the steps involved in
laser scanning-based data collection:
• Scanner setup: The laser scanner is set up in a position
from which it can capture the desired area. The scanner
parameters, such as resolution and range, are set accord-
ing to the required level of detail.
• Laser scanning: The laser scanner emits laser light that
is directed towards the target. The laser light reflects off
the target and is captured by the scanner. The scanner
measures the time it takes for the laser light to reflect
back to the scanner, which is used to calculate the dis-
tance to the target.
• Point cloud generation: The scanner generates a point
cloud [81] that represents the 3D coordinates of the tar-

• Data processing: The point cloud is processed to

• Model generation: A 3D model is generated from the

There are various kinds of laser scanners, including
ground-based, aerial, and mobile models. Terrestrial scan-
ners are used to detect modest to medium-sized items and
settings, and they are often tripod-mounted. Large areas,
such woods or coasts, can be scanned using airborne scan-
ners, which are placed aboard aircraft. Roads, bridges, and
other forms of transportation infrastructure are scanned using
mobile scanners that are placed on moving vehicles. With
many variants (section II-B), photogrammetry and laser
scanning is one of the most crucial techniques to col-
lect photos for further consideration in digitizing cultural
heritages.
While digital image capturing consists of different stages
of photo capturing, PH extracts as many features as it can
extract from a 3D structure by capturing photos from different
angles, lighting conditions, and other aspects.
• Terrestrial: This approach [84] motivates capturing
photos from one location on air or ground with a hand-
held camera or tripod.
• Aerial: This type of PH covers a much larger area than
the previous one [85]. It is mainly captured from a
flying aircraft (e.g., Satellite imagery) or a camera in
the air [86]. Aerial imagery can further be divided into
vertical and oblique PH based on the axis of the camera.
While many cameras are available in the market, this
method includes lightweight ones for capturing mean-
ingful photographs.
• Close Range: The range is an important aspect while
capturing the images. While Aerial photography covers
more area capturing the image from a far distance, close-
range photogrammetry [87], [88], [89] considers taking
the images from a lesser range from the terrain. This
technique is useful when preserving minute details in the
architecture rather than covering more parts.
• Stereophotogrammetry: This special PH method
involves determining 3D coordinates from one or mul-
tiple 2D image coordinates taken from different posi-
tions [90].
Starting with the idea of physical model creation from 2D
images nowadays motivates a plethora of software that can
work with 3D reconstruction from 2D images. The work of
2D image capturing includes photogrammetric approaches
in various cultural heritage sites and architectures. This
method manipulates the camera parameters (focal length,
adjusting lighting conditions, principal component), result-
ing in non-linear solutions to extracting homologous coordi-
nates [91].
C. VRML MODEL OF VICTORIA SQUARE
[8] refers to various laser scanning techniques on Victoria
Square in Gorizia, Italy, to reconstruct a 3D city model. The
efficiency of aerial laser scanning allows the acquisition of

to the subjected architecture. The method also includes an
effective segmentation algorithm by filtering Digital Ter-

additional condition variables like Digital Elevation Model

• Feature Extraction: The SFM algorithm retrieves and
extracts distinctive features from the images, such as
corners or edges, at this step. The identical pixels from
different photos are then matched using these attributes.
• Camera pose estimation: The SFM algorithm calcu-
lates the camera pose and intrinsics (such as focal length

IV. INPAINTING: A TRADITIONAL AND DEEP-BASED
APPROACH
A. DIGITAL INPAINTING
Image inpainting [33], [161] is an image completion tech-
nique used to restore damaged images, to remove or restore
some portions of any image with no visually perceptible
difference.
Image inpainting finds crucial applications in the digi-
tal preservation and restoration of cultural heritage artifacts
like murals, old paintings, manuscripts, documents, and pho-
tographs. These artifacts experience contusion and degrada-
tion over time or may get damaged due to scratches and folds.
In such cases, inpainting is used to reverse the effect of time
and artificially restore their undamaged version by recovering
the damages and filling up holes or missing parts.
Technically speaking, inpainting is estimating missing
pixel values in the damaged using the pixel information
from the surrounding areas. Some approaches consider the
immediate surroundings for this prediction, while others look
for portions in the image that have the highest similarity to
the neighborhood of the damaged section. Mathematically,
inpainting is an ill-posed inverse problem since there can be
many possible solutions, i.e., reconstructions corresponding
to each image. Algorithms differ from each other depending
on how realistically they can fill up the missing parts.
Inpainting methods can be broadly categorized into two
classes: 1. Traditional approaches 2. DL-induced approaches.

1) Traditional
approaches:
Traditional
inpainting
approaches are suitable for filling small regions in
images, like cracks and small holes. These techniques
do not require training over the dataset but are unsuit-
able for images with large missing portions. The initial
application of digital inpainting was made for image
sequences or films [162], where missing regions in a
frame are interpolated using information from adja-
cent frames. Later texture-based inpainting was pro-
posed where a given area is filled with user-defined
texture [163]. The first algorithm for image inpaint-
ing without any user intervention was proposed by
Bertalmio et al. [33]. Traditional inpainting techniques
can be broadly organized into five classes.
•
Diffusion-based
algorithm
proposed
by
Bertalmio et al. [33] used discretized version of the
anisotropic diffusion equation to fill up structured
regions having different backgrounds. This approach
attempts to imitate the manual inpainting methods
through an iterative process. The central concept here
is to smoothly propagate information from the neigh-
borhood into the whole region along the direction of
‘isophotes,’ i.e., The direction of minimal intensity
changes and is obtained by computing the perpendicu-
lar gradient vector at every pixel. Another variation was
proposed [164], where the Navier-Stokes equations
of fluid dynamics govern the information propaga-
tion along isophotes, and the flow of incompressible
fluid models the image intensity. Chan [165] proposed
a total variation (TV) method for image inpainting,
where a total variational norm-based energy function
is minimized. This method is further improved [166]
by allowing curvature-dependent diffusion (CDD) for
clean and noisy non-textured images. Here, nonlinear
second and third-order PDE-based methods for inpaint-
ing to overcome the limitations of linear interpolations.
These PDE-based methods are suitable for inpainting
small regions and thin cracks.
• Texture synthesis-based methods, also called patch-
based synthesis, pick up sample textures from clear
sections of the image and replicate them in the dam-
aged region, maintaining the color relation between the
newly constructed region and its neighbourhood. Ružić
and Pižurica [167] proposed a context-aware texture-
based inpainting method. Here the image is divided
into blocks of variable size based on their context,
and the approach searches non-local image regions
for well-matched patches. The process uses Markov
Random Field (MRF) prior, i.e., a priori knowledge
about the consistency of neighbouring image patches
modelled using MRF. Kawai et al. [168] proposed an
inpainting method based on diminished reality, a back-
ground geometry-dependent gap-filling method. This
approach approximates the background geometry of
any object by combining the local planes and incorpo-
rating perspective correction of textures. Some inpaint-
ing approaches use a low-rank prior [169],i.e., any raw
image converted into a low-rank matrix owing to the
non-local self-similarity. Thus the inpainting method
boils down to appropriate singular value truncation.
Lu et al. [170] use low-rank approximation on hori-
zontal and vertical image gradients. Zeng et al. [171]
proposed an inpainting method that uses a saliency map
and color information to locate the best matching patch
in the image.
• Exemplar-based methods combine diffusion-based
and texture-based inpainting advantages. The approach
was first proposed by Criminisi et al. [172]. The algo-
rithm first divides the image into two segments: the
target region, which is to be inpainted, and the source
region, which is the rest of the image. Samples from
the source region are selected and used to fill the target
region. The filling-up process starts at the contour of
the target region and moves inwards to the target. For
each pixel on the contour, a fixed-size patch is con-
structed, having the pixel at its center. Then for each
patch, a priority is calculated based on a confidence
value, which measures the ratio of available pixels to
total pixels in a patch, and a data value, which mea-
sures the strength of isophotes in that patch. Having
determined the priorities, the highest priority patch is
filled up first, using a patch from the source region with
the highest similarity with the target patch. In [172],
the authors have used the sum of square difference.
After filling up every patch, the confidence values are
recalculated, and the iteration proceeds. The author has
demonstrated that filling orders are crucial for faithful
inpainting. The exemplar-based methods cannot prop-
erly reconstruct multiple textures and geometric line
segments. [173].
Different modifications of this basic exemplar-based
approach have been developed by adopting differ-
ent methods for calculating the data values, confi-
dence values, and patch priority [174], [175], [176]
and employing different search strategies [177], [178].
Hung et al. [179] proposed an improved method by
first applying mean-shift segmentation to extract the
contours, then repairing the broken contours by using
Bezier curves, and finally applying the traditional
exemplar-based inpainting. Another approach [180]
uses weighted aggregation of multiple non-local
neighborhoods that exploits the redundant informa-
tion present in any image to generate better quality
results. Fan et al. [181] used an adaptive patch search-
ing method by changing patch sizes depending on
patch sparsity and picking up the most similar rotation
invariant patch matching the target patch. Perceptu-
ally aware inpainting [182] is another modification of
exemplar-based inpainting that uses perceptual-fidelity
aware mean squared error (PAMSE) [183] to measure

2) Deep-based Inpainting: DL-induced inpainting meth-
ods [192], [193], [194] are popular with the advent
of various promising DL algorithms and architec-
tures in computer vision and image processing. These
approaches outperform traditional inpainting methods
when the damage in the images is enormous. Here
DL-based approaches are categorized based on the net-
work architectures used.
• CNN-based approaches: Pathak et al. [195] proposed
a context encoder, which is an encoder-decoder type
architecture for reconstructing the missing region of
any image based on its learned features. The encoder
is based on the AlexNet [60] architecture having
channel-wise fully connected layers. The authors used
a composite loss function comprised of l2 norm-based
reconstruction loss and GAN-based adversarial loss.
Here, the encoder encodes the image and the missing
region into a suitable latent space, and the decoder
attempts to reconstruct it using upsampling. Thus the
features learned from the image segment help recon-
struct the masked region. In [196], the authors have
proposed a blind inpainting convolutional neural net-
work (BICNN), which can reconstruct the damaged
image without prior knowledge about the location
inpainted. The network uses convolution layers with
zero padding and ReLU activation with no pooling
or fully-connected layers. The network is trained on
preprocessed ImageNet dataset by minimizing MSE
loss. Sasaki et al. [197] proposed a CNN-based net-
work for automatically identifying and completing the
gaps without any user intervention for line drawing
images. The network consists of zero-padded convo-
lution layers with ReLU and upsampling layers.
Input images are grayscale line drawings of any size,
and generated output is the same size as the input.
Yang et al. [198] proposed an encoder-decoder CNN
network using a loss function containing three terms: a
holistic content term, a local texture term, and a TV-loss
term. There is an encoder-decoder type content predic-
tion network, which employs a combination of l2 loss
and adversarial loss to learn to predict the content of
the removed region of the input image. A pretrained
VGG-19 is used as a texture network, and the outputs of
relu3_1 and relu4_1 layers are combined with learning
local textures. The TV-loss term invokes smoothness in
the reconstruction.
Moreover, the authors have proposed a multi-scale
neural network for patch synthesis in high-resolution
images. A U-Net-based architecture with a shift oper-
ator is proposed in [199]. The network is named Shift-
Net. Its shift operator in the encoder helps to recon-
struct the missing parts using the features learned from
the known region. For training, the authors employed
a combination of reconstruction loss, adversarial loss,
and guidance loss, which the shift operator incurs.
The guidance loss is the squared norm of the dif-
ference between the ground truth image’s encoded
feature and the recovered image’s decoder feature.
The corresponding encoder and decoder layers are
connected via the shift operation. Artist net [200] is
also an encoder-decoder type model that separately
extracts and compares the style features and content
features of the ground truth image and the synthesized
image. It used an intricate loss function to ensure the
reconstructed image’s content and style consistency.
The loss function contains two terms: a reconstruction
loss comprising of l1 loss between reconstructed and
ground-truth image and perceptual loss [201]; a latent
loss containing content loss and style loss in latent
dimension and finally, an adversarial loss computed
using a PatchGAN discriminator.

A GAN is trained on a set of uncorrupted images

masked image. Finally, the generator uses the latent
vector zi to reconstruct an image that corresponds to
the reconstructed image. To measure the closeness of
the latent vectors, the authors used a loss function
comprising of a content loss and prior loss. The content
loss is the weighted l1 distance between the corrupted
image and generator output with zi; the prior loss,
which ensures that the generated image lies in the same
manifold, is the GAN loss used during discrimina-

residual blocks are incorporated in both generator and
discriminator of the WGAN 2. the fully connected lay-

gradient of the masked image and the gradient of the
recovered image. Jboor et al. [205] followed this same

However, that generated image may not be a convincing
and realistic one. As an alternative, an architecture

proposed, which can generate several reconstructions
consistent with the source image. The network uses
a style extractor and feeds the masked image and the
style vector to the generator’s input. It also employs two
discriminators for patch-based and global prediction.
The effectivity of the architecture is studied on CelebA,
PlantVillage, and MauFlex datasets.

• Image inpainting can be used for image forgery

image. This approach combines central pixel mapping

noise addition, and many more. Although in-painted

in-painted patch and neighboring regions are disturbed.

joint probability density matrix of the image pixels.

a set of features based on the changes’ intra-channel
and inter-channel local variations.

inpainting probability for each pixel in an image. The
network is trained with MIT place dataset [219] with

dataset [220].

V. 3D SEMANTIC SEGMENTATION: MULTI-VIEW AND

Segmentation on 2-dimensional images motivates extracting
meaningful artifacts from the picture by calculating prob-

cessing approaches [147], [223]. On the other hand, multi-
view geometry better use multiple camera scenes to observe
a 3-dimensional spatial relationship of calibrated images
engaging numerous viewpoints. Connecting segmentation
and multi-view geometry can be a well-defined work to
support concrete architecture-based point cloud detection
expressing the background and subjective object from the
scene. This part of the survey discusses some crucial 3D
segmentation approaches with multi-camera segmentation
for motion-sensing images and videos without creating a
dense 3D reconstruction algorithm. It further explains the 3D
segmentation of meaningful objects rather than 2D, imposing
more significant results.
Multi-view object segmentation is an advantageous task
to be addressed using 3D spatial segmentation, engaging
several viewpoints rather than limited resource monocu-
lar approaches [46]. The method uses variational inference
to improve the applicability of the sparse modelling to
combine GMM (Gaussian Mixture models). Next, they
propose a depth estimation, minimizing the energy map-
ping using colour-based segmentation to extract multiple
Regions of Interest between foreground and background.
Time series estimation can be a vital scenario considering
further improvement of object tracking from motion pictures.
Thus, numerous viewpoints are engaged to make a better

C. PointNet
Convolutional architectures require regular input data formats
such as 3D voxels or image grids to perform operations like
kernel optimizations. Point clouds represented by a set of 3D
points {Pi|i = 1, 2, . . . , n} have an irregular format. Since
they are an essential data type, many researchers transform
point clouds into 3D voxel grids. This transformation renders
an unnecessary increase in data volume. Qi et al. in [45]
designed a novel neural network to handle this. This net-
work, named PointNet, considered point clouds as input well,
respecting the permutation invariance of the points. Each
point has been represented by three coordinates (x, y, z), and
additional dimensions can be added from the computation of
normals or other features. PointNet uses a single symmetric
function, max pooling, which is quite effective yet simple.
On learning a set of optimization functions, the network
selects some points of interest in the point cloud and encodes
the reason for such selection. The input is either sampled from
a shape directly or a part is segmented from a 3D scene. The
final fully connected layer aggregates into the global descrip-
tor, the optimal learned values for obtaining the entire shape.
The output for each n point and m semantic subcategory will
be m × n score. It is convenient to apply rigid or affine
transformations to the input format of this network as each
point transforms independently. Also, an advantage of this
network is that it learns summarizing of an input point cloud

D. RANDOM FOREST BASED POINT CLOUD EXTRACTION

As 3D point cloud information become more readily avail-

extraction is particularly pertinent in the field of heritage data
preservation since it enables the identification and preserva-

a number of research articles have examined the use of
random forests to the extraction of point clouds [45] from
heritage data. In the sections that follow, we will evaluate a
few of these works and their significance.
1) AUTOMATIC SEGMENTATION OF POINT CLOUD DATA
[248] presents a new method for segmentation of point cloud
data information for cultural heritage sites. The research
provides an automatic segmentation method for point clouds
of cultural heritage sites based on random forests. The pro-
posed approach takes use of a set of geometric, radiometric,
and contextual data for each pixel, as well as a hierarchical
segmentation scheme based on an adaptive octree structure.
The findings indicate that the suggested strategy outperforms
other approaches in terms of segmentation accuracy.
2) POINT CLOUD CLASSIFICATION BY MEANS OF RANDOM
FOREST AND SVM
A comparison between random forests and support vector
machines for the classification of point clouds of historical
buildings is demonstrated in [249]. The technique divide
points into numerous classes, including such roof, wall, and
windows, using morphological and radiometric properties.
The study demonstrates that in regards to classification preci-

3) ML BASED APPROACH ON EGYPTIAN BASALT QUARRIES
This study on ancient Egyptian basalt quarries in [250] sug-
gests utilising LiDAR data to classify using a random forest

LiDAR point clouds to reduce noise. Then they take informa-

Kappa coefficient are some of the criteria used by the authors
to assess its success. Results indicate that the suggested strat-
egy outperformed more conventional approaches like visual
interpretation, with an overall accuracy of 93.7%.
The proposed method is innovative because it classifies
ancient Egyptian basalt quarries using machine learning
methods. The work highlights the possibilities of analyzing
and categorizing archaeological features using LiDAR data
and machine learning techniques.

B. PROBABILISTIC METHODS
We obtain cultural relics mostly in fragments, which need
reassembly to make them worthwhile for cultural heritage
studies. However, automatic reassembling fragments is not
very easy as the geometric shapes of the fragments may not
be known. A novel probabilistic method for reassembling
fragments has been proposed by Wang et al. in [281]. In this
method, 2D link-chain and 3D spatial-description descriptors
are used to detect matching units of the fragments, and then
the reassembly probability pairwise is calculated. We demon-
strate the efficiency of the method using eight challenging
datasets.
Data from point clouds of historical places are extracted
and analysed using a method called probabilistic modeling-
based point cloud extraction. It entails precisely identifying
and categorising various point cloud data items, such as
structures, trees, and terrain, using statistical techniques and
machine learning algorithms. This method makes it possible
to analyse historical sites more effectively and accurately,
which can aid in preservation efforts, archaeological study,
and other related sectors. Researchers can better comprehend
the location and its history by evaluating the structure and
makeup of the point cloud data, which will help them make
better judgements about how to conserve and analyse it.
1) AUTOMATIC RECONSTRUCTION OF HERITAGE SITE FROM
LiDAR DATA
A new probabilistic approach for reconstructing building
roofs from airborne LiDAR data is demonstrated in [282].
By using elevation and point density to identify proba-
ble roof points, the proposed technique then generates a
three-dimensional roof model by fitting a probabilistic plane
model to the determined roof points. The method was evalu-
ated on three various datasets and yielded promising results
when compared to other cutting-edge techniques.
2) POSE ESTIMATION AND PROBABILISTIC 3D OBJECT
RECOGNITION
In this study [283], point cloud data is used to suggest a
probabilistic method for 3D object detection and posture
estimation in cluttered situations. To estimate the object’s
posture, the method combines a probabilistic model of the
scene and a probabilistic model of the object’s shape and
appearance. The method was tested using real-world datasets,
and even in congested settings with occlusions and partial
views, it produced good results.

Restoration of cultural heritage is a critical aspect of preserv-
ing cultural assets. One important aspect for preserving the

An intelligent sensor [285], [286], [287] is also an efficient

A recurrent neural network empirically evaluated with

from the bridge continuously.
In the construction sector, the adoption of AI has sev-

real-time inpainting using video inpainting [297] that
restores the broken tiles of a building.
XI. CONCLUSION
The restoration of digital heritage is mesmerizing, which
guides to recreating history using futuristic plans. The arti-
cle strives to explain and discuss some well-known works
that can be applied to heritage restoration. We infer 3D
reconstruction as an essential task pursued by various classes
based on complexity for restoring broken furs of inheritance.
Nonetheless, mechanization in the field still holds numer-
ous unanswered questions. Hence, first, we chose to com-
mence with some of the well-known resolvable scenarios
using some of the popular dataset collection methods [6],
[69]. Such methods result in unique datasets, e.g., [21], [22],
[23], [58], which can be used for self-regulating training
approaches even further. We use DL and image processing
approaches to mark their capability on artistic legacy, fol-
lowed by GAN, BIM, and discriminating case studies of
some popular architectures worldwide. This paper presents
several holistic approaches to data collection, computer
vision, point cloud, and genetic algorithm-based segmented
analysis of heritage image data. We then proceed with an
exhaustive analysis of the papers in this survey and con-
clude with potential research direction on restoring cultural
heritages.
